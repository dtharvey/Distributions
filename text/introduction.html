\(\require{mhchem}\)The plots on the right show the distribution of results for four systems. Each plot shows the frequency of an outcome on the y-axis as a function of possible outcomes on the x-axis. Each distribution has a different shape and structure, and, therefore, a different mathematical form. Before continuing, take a moment to make note of the similarities and differences in these distributions.

Copper/Sulfur Ratio in Copper Sulfide. If you mix powdered copper metal and powdered sulfur and ignite them in a crucible, copper (I) sulfide forms with an empirical formula of CuxS. The value of x is determined by weighing the Cu before ignition and then determining the mass of CuxS when the reaction is complete; any excess sulfur is removed as \(\ce{SO2}\). The plot in the upper-left corner shows the distribution of values of x for 62 trials.

Emission of Beta Particles from 40K. In beta emission, an unstable neutron decays into an electron and a proton. The proton remains in the nucleus, which increases the atom’s atomic number by one; the electron is ejected as a beta particle. The plot in the upper-right corner shows the distribution of the number of beta particles emitted from a single sample of 40K during each of 365 consecutive 7-s intervals.

Certification of Class A 10-mL Pipettes. A class A pipette delivers a calibrated volume of liquid. Each pipet has a unique identification number and carries a certificate with its precise calibrated volume traced to a NIST-approved standard (NIST is the National Institute of Standards and Technology). The plot in the lower-left corner shows the distribution of calibrated volumes for 1000 Class A 10-mL pipettes.

13C Atoms in Cholesterol. Carbon has two stable isotopes: 12C and 13C. Because 12C is much more abundant than 13C, most organic molecules have no atoms of 13C; a few molecules, however, will contain one or more atoms of 13C. The plot in the lower-right corner shows the distribution of the number of 13C atoms in 100 molecules of cholesterol.
