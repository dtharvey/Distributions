The Poisson Distribution: Emission of Beta Particles from 40K

Exercise 1. The figure on the right is a barplot of the number of beta particles emitted by a sample of 40K during 365 consecutive 7-s intervals. Although a barplot and a histogram look similar, they are not interchangeable. A histogram is used for continuous data and a barplot is used for discrete data. Why are the results of beta emission discrete instead of continuous?

Exercise 2. This experiment follows a Poisson distribution in which the probability that a particular number of beta particles is emitted in a fixed period of time, \(p(x)\), is \[p(x) = \frac{\lambda^x e^{-\lambda}}{x!}\] where \(\lambda\) is the average rate of beta emission and where the exclamation point is a factorial (e.g. \(3! = 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6\)). For the CuxS data we drew the theoretical distribution as a smooth line, but here we show a set of points connected by line segments. Explain why we can display the normal distribution as a smooth line but cannot do so with a Poisson distribution.

Exercise 3. How does the shape of the Poisson distribution change as you adjust \(\lambda\) from 0 to 13? Why does this change in shape make sense given the equation for the Poisson distribution? Adjust the slider to determine the value of \(\lambda\) for this data. Use your value of \(\lambda\) to calculate the probability that a single beta particle is emitted. Explain how to convert this probability into a number of events and compare this result to the data in the figure.

Exercise 4. The mean for a Poisson distribution is \(\lambda\) and its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\lambda}\). Report the mean and standard deviation for this data using your value for \(\lambda\) and then download the data as a spreadsheet and check your result by calculating the actual mean and standard deviation.
